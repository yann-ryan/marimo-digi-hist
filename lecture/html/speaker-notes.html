Part 1 · Why count words?

3 Why count words?

The big picture before any method: what text analysis is for, what it is called, what it can and can't do.

4 Why do text analysis?

Five reasons. Scale: the Old Bailey Proceedings are about 123 million words, roughly three years of reading at eight hours a day; the Royal Society's journals another 64 million. Change: some patterns exist only across many texts, e.g. when 'electric fire' disappears. Comparison: what is typical of one group, period or place, compared with another. Finding: search and concordance point you to the few texts worth reading closely. Checking: a historian's generalisation can be tested against the whole record, and the test can be repeated.

5 Close and distant reading

Close reading: Benjamin Franklin's letter to Peter Collinson, dated Philadelphia 1 October 1752 and read at the Royal Society on 21 December 1752 (Philosophical Transactions 47). How to make a kite that draws 'the electric fire' from a thunder-cloud; it claims to demonstrate that lightning and electricity are the same. For Franklin electricity is a fire: matter you can draw off, store in a Leyden jar and use to light spirits. Distant reading: 'electric fire' and 'electric current' per million words in the Royal Society's journals, 25-year periods. 'Electric fire' peaks in Franklin's time and is gone by 1800; 'electric current' takes off after 1825 (Volta's pile 1800, Ørsted 1820, Faraday). The letter shows what electricity was to Franklin; the counts show how long that idea was in use. The lecture combines reading and counting in this way throughout.

6 Computers and historians since the 1940s

Busa: the Index Thomisticus, the first large-scale humanities computing project, built with IBM. Cliometrics: econometrics applied to history, e.g. Fogel and Engerman's Time on the Cross; big claims, big controversies. 1980s backlash: the linguistic and cultural turn, Foucault, microhistories like Ginzburg's The Cheese and the Worms. 1990s–2000s: mass digitisation and tools to analyse language. What is different now is that historians count the words in their sources as well as numerical data.

7 Names for the field

The same family of methods travels under different names. Corpus linguistics (from the 1960s): language in large, designed corpora; gave us collocation and keyness. Humanities computing, later digital humanities: from Busa onwards. Distant reading: Franco Moretti's term (2000) for studying literature through patterns across thousands of books. Culturomics: Michel et al. (2011), Google Books and the Ngram Viewer. Cultural analytics: Lev Manovich and others, culture as data. NLP and text mining: the computer-science toolbox. For historians: digital or computational history. Reading: Moretti, Distant Reading (2013); Ted Underwood, Distant Horizons (2019); Jo Guldi, The Dangerous Art of Text Mining (2023).

8 A map of methods

An overview of what is available; we do four today. Search and concordance: find every occurrence and read it in context. Frequency and n-grams: how often, and when. Collocation and keyness: which words keep company, and what distinguishes one set of texts from another (MI, TF-IDF). Word embeddings: meaning from context, and how it shifts. Not today: topic models (LDA; what are these texts about?), named entities and places (who and where; geoparsing), text reuse (who copied whom; e.g. passim, used to trace news across newspapers), classification (sorting texts into categories you define, with supervised machine learning, including sentiment), and large language models (summarising, extracting, asking questions of a corpus; powerful but hard to check). All of them start from the same move: turning text into countable units.

9 The research process

The research loop, with today's electricity example under each step. Question: what was electricity to the people who studied it? Corpus: the Royal Society's journals, 1665–1920. Prepare: lower-case, split into words, treat 'electric' and 'electrical' as one. Count: which noun follows 'electric' in each period. Read: go back to Franklin, Cavendish, Faraday. Interpret: electricity changes from a substance to a measured quantity. The results usually lead to a revised question. Every method today belongs to the counting step; the other steps depend on historical knowledge.

10 What counting can and can’t do

Text analysis is good at scale, comparison and pattern, and at pointing you to where to read. It cannot tell you why something changed, cannot read irony, tone or context, cannot see what was never written down, kept or digitised, and does not replace close reading. Keep this list in mind for Part 6.

Part 2 · What are we counting?

12 What are we counting?

Before any method: what is in the corpus and how it was made.

13 From scan to text

Real example from Delpher: the Noordhollandsche courant, 3 May 1780, page 2, appointments and deaths in the VOC's Asian settlements. Top: the scan. Bottom: the OCR text Delpher serves for the same lines; errors light up in red. The long s in 'ontslagen' (dismissed) is read as f, so a search for 'ontslagen' misses this article; italic names suffer most (Gale → Gak, Jaffanapatnam → Jaiknapataaai); e is read as c (October → Octobcr). OCR for print (Delpher), HTR for handwriting (VOC and notarial archives, Amsterdam City Archives): always ask what the machine did to the text before you count it.

14 Preparing a corpus

Select a cohesive corpus; describe it with metadata; clean it (OCR correction, spelling standardisation); prepare it (split into words, lemmatise: stole → steal, tag parts of speech). Each step is optional and each is a choice that changes every count that follows.

15 The Old Bailey Proceedings

Printed accounts of trials at London's central criminal court, 1674–1913, digitised and marked up by Old Bailey Online (Hitchcock, Shoemaker, Emsley, Howard, McLaughlin et al.). Every trial is tagged by hand with offence, verdict, punishment and defendant's gender. In the lab we use a random sample of 12,482 trials (up to 500 per decade); the word embeddings are trained on the full text. Data via Hugging Face biglam/old_bailey_proceedings, CC BY-NC 4.0.

16 The Royal Society’s journals

The Royal Society Corpus, built at Saarland University from the Philosophical Transactions and Proceedings of the Royal Society of London. Open version 6.0.4: 17,520 texts, 1665–1920: letters, papers, abstracts, with author, journal, year and topic. The corpus of the second lab notebook. A source with a history: the Transactions began as Henry Oldenburg's newsletter of letters and reports and became a professional scientific journal.

17 The Dutch parliament

The proceedings of both chambers of the Dutch parliament (Tweede Kamer and Eerste Kamer), 1814–2012, from the PoliticalMashup project (Maarten Marx and others), published through DANS under CC0. About 51,000 sittings. Each speech has a date, chamber, speaker, role (member, minister, chair) and, for about two thirds of speeches, a party. The lab uses a sample of 10,648 speeches and two word2vec models, for 1814–1917 and 1918–2012, each trained on about 27 million words. Like the other two corpora it is a source with a history: spelling reforms (zoo → zo, mensch → mens), OCR errors in older volumes, and early members who sometimes spoke French.

18 What counts as a word?

Tokenisation: the exact rule used in the lab: lower-case everything and keep runs of letters a–z. Lost: numbers (40), punctuation, the apostrophe in Prosecutor's (the red tokens). Kept apart: murder and murther, shilling and shilings. Students edit this sentence in the lab and watch the tokens change.

Part 3 · Frequency

19 How often? Frequency

Method 1: term frequency.

20 Method 1: frequency

The method card: the same four questions for each method. Frequency counts words or phrases and divides by the size of the corpus (per 10,000 or per million words), so that periods or groups of different size can be compared. Good for trends, events, the rise and fall of terms and concepts. Watch out: the corpus changes over time; spelling and OCR; words with several meanings.

21 Raw and normalised counts

Same Delpher search for 'liberaal', two views. The raw count partly measures how much was printed and digitised: more newspapers later, so raw counts rise anyway. The relative frequency asks: of everything printed that year, how much mentions the word?

22 The most frequent words

Counts from the Old Bailey lab sample (6.8 million words). Stopwords: very common words (the, and, of), often removed. 'prisoner' and 'mr' tell us about the genre; 'q' (Question in the transcripts) and 'th' (what is left of '29th') about how it was printed. Stopword lists are choices too: removing 'she' or 'her' would hide gender.

23 N-grams: words in sequence

A window slides along the text: every pair of neighbours is a bigram (2-gram), every three a trigram. N-grams separate meanings: 'watch' the timepiece versus 'the watch' and 'watch house', the night watch. Bigram counts from 1,000 trials containing 'watch' in the Old Bailey sample. Next slide: an n-gram that tells a history.

24 The noun after ‘electric’, 1725–1924

Every 'electric' or 'electrical' in the Royal Society's journals, and the noun that follows (a bigram), per million words in each 25-year period. The table fills in period by period. 1725–49: a 'virtue' or 'effluvia' given off by rubbed glass (Stephen Gray, Desaguliers). 1750–99: a 'fluid' or 'fire' (Franklin, Cavendish), increasingly a 'spark' and a 'shock' (the Leyden jar). The 'electric organ' of the torpedo fish appears in 1773 (Walsh, Hunter). After Volta's pile (1800) and electromagnetism (Ørsted 1820, Faraday) the 'electric current' dominates. Late in the century 'resistance' (measurement), 'light' (electric lighting), and finally 'field' and 'waves' (Maxwell, Hertz, wireless). Of the three questions, this one is mostly 'its meaning changed'.

25 Keyword in context

A concordance (keyword in context, KWIC) is the oldest digital humanities tool: Busa's Index Thomisticus was a concordance. One line for each stage. Franklin 1752: fire drawn from clouds. Cavendish 1771: an elastic fluid. Hunter 1773: the electric organ of the torpedo, an anatomical object. Faraday 1832: a current you can reverse. Fleming 1902: electric waves and Marconi's 'space telegraphy'. Other stages: Desaguliers' 'electrical Virtue' (1739), Ellicott's 'electric Effluvia' (1748), Siemens' 'electric light' (1879).

26 Frequency in practice: Chagall in German and English books

Michel et al., 'Quantitative analysis of culture using millions of digitized books', Science 331 (2011), the paper behind the Google Books Ngram Viewer. The Jewish painter Marc Chagall, labelled 'degenerate' by the Nazi regime: in German-language books his name falls through the 1930s to almost nothing in 1940–42 and recovers after 1945; in English-language books it keeps rising. The English line serves as a comparison. Note the German fall starts in the late 1920s; and 'books' means books Google scanned from a handful of libraries. Data re-downloaded from the Ngram Viewer (2019 corpora, 1-year smoothing).

Part 4 · Association

29 In what company? Association

Method 2: association.

30 Method 2: association

Association looks at the words around a word (collocation) or compares one set of texts with another (keyness, distinctive words, TF-IDF). Good for how a concept is framed, and what distinguishes groups, periods or authors. Watch out: scores like MI favour rare words; the result depends on what you compare with; being near a word is not the same as meaning it.

31 Collocation

How often does word X occur near word Y? Count every word inside a window (here up to 5 words either side of 'gin'), across the whole corpus; then ask which turn up far more often than chance (next slide). Example sentence from an Old Bailey trial of 1776 (t17760911-88). The strongest collocates of gin in the sample: quartern (a quarter pint), glass, drank, pint, beer.

32 Observed vs expected: the MI score

Mutual information: log2 of observed over expected, where expected is how often the two words would meet by chance given how common each is. 1 means twice as often as chance, 3 eight times. MI rewards rare words, so set a minimum number of meetings.

33 Words near ‘electricity’, 1725–1924

Collocation over time: every word within 5 words of 'electric', 'electrical' or 'electricity', sorted into hand-made groups, as a share of all occurrences in each 25-year period. Rubbing & attraction: rubbed, friction, attract, repel, effluvia, virtue, amber, wax. Fluid & fire: fluid, fire, aether. Living bodies: animal, torpedo, gymnotus, eel, fish, nerve, muscle, frog, patient, paralytic, cure, disease, shock. Machines & technology: machine, battery, dynamo, telegraph, lamp, lighting, cable, engine, motor, tramways. Current & measurement: current, resistance, conductivity, volt, ohm, potential, units. Living bodies are a real strand (medical cures by electricity in the 1750s–70s; the torpedo and the electric eel in the 1770s; Galvani's frogs in the 1790s), at most 8 in 100, fading after 1900. Machines stay below 7 in 100 in these journals. Measurement words rise to about 20 in 100 from the 1820s. The groups are my choice: a different list would give a different picture. Discuss.

34 Association in practice: ‘liberty from’ and ‘liberty to’

Isaiah Berlin, 'Two Concepts of Liberty' (1958): negative liberty is freedom from interference; positive liberty is freedom to act, to govern oneself. Which did early modern writers mean? John Regan turns this into a collocation question: which preposition follows the word? Counts from Regan, Semantic Change and Collective Knowledge in 18th-Century Britain (Bloomsbury 2023), built on ECCO (the earliest periods presumably EEBO; check). Raw counts: liberty from 234 / 1,272 / 2,791 / 5,934; liberty to 3,143 / 19,913 / 68,788 / 154,122; freedom from 1,285 / 431 / 5,890 / 15,667; freedom to 511 / 189 / 5,639 / 12,597. The chart shows 'from' as a share of from + to. The two words are used differently: 'freedom' is more often followed by 'from', 'liberty' by 'to'. Part 6 returns to this example: in practice 'liberty to' often means permission.

35 TF-IDF

Term frequency times inverse document frequency. tf: how often the word occurs in this document, here per 1,000 words. N: the number of documents. df: the number of documents that contain the word. idf = log(N / df) is zero for a word that occurs in every document, and larger the fewer documents contain it. The product is high for words that are frequent in this document and found in few others. We use the natural logarithm; the base only changes the scale.

36 TF-IDF: killing trials

Documents: the nine Old Bailey offence categories in the lab sample (killing, theft, deception, coining and so on). Left: the ten most frequent words in all killing trials together. They are the same function words as in every other category, so they say nothing about killing. Right: the ten words with the highest TF-IDF score in the killing trials. These describe homicide trials: the deceased, the coroner's inquest (an inquisition), manslaughter, a mortal wound. Some are surnames (sellers, yates, serne) from a few long trials: TF-IDF also picks out words that are frequent in one document only because one long text in it uses them a lot. 'deceas' is a tokenising or OCR fragment of 'deceased'.

37 What counts as a document?

TF-IDF compares documents, and the researcher decides what a document is. It can be a single trial, all the trials for one offence, all the trials of women and all the trials of men, all the trials in one decade, or in the Royal Society Corpus all the papers by one author or in one journal. The choice of document decides which comparison TF-IDF makes, and so which question it answers.

38 TF-IDF by offence category

Each Old Bailey offence category treated as one document; three of the words with the highest TF-IDF scores in each (surnames left out). 'Royal offences' in the Old Bailey are mostly coining.

39 Distinctive words: trials of women and of men

Defendant's gender as tagged by Old Bailey Online: 3,006 trials of women, 8,919 of men. log2 ratio of the share of trials mentioning each word. Women: clothing (stays, petticoats, quilted), 'spinster' as a legal description, pregnancy and the midwife (infanticide trials). Men: driving, horses, fraud, 'carnally' from rape trials. 'More common in trials of women' does not mean 'women said this'. Content note: real trials, including sexual violence and infanticide.

40 Distinctiveness in practice: violence in the Old Bailey

Klingenstein, Hitchcock and DeDeo, 'The civilizing process in London's Old Bailey', PNAS 111 (2014): 9419–24. Same corpus as ours. They measure, year by year, how different the vocabulary of trials for violent offences is from that of non-violent ones (Jensen-Shannon divergence: a distinctiveness score for two whole vocabularies). Blue: violent vs non-violent. Red: arbitrary groupings of offences (the baseline). Around 1780 the two kinds of trial sound alike; by 1900 they are sharply distinct. The authors read this as a slow change in attitudes to violence (Norbert Elias's 'civilizing process'), which they find in the language of the trials. Discussion question: does this reflect a change in violence or in the court's record-keeping?

Part 5 · Embeddings

42 What does it mean? Embeddings

Method 3: semantics. One running example for most of this section: gin, beer, pot, watch, handkerchief. Every number comes from the Old Bailey sample or the lab's models.

43 Method 3: word embeddings

Embeddings turn each word into a list of numbers learned from the contexts it appears in, so that words used in similar contexts get similar numbers. Good for finding similar words, exploring a vocabulary, and tracing how meaning shifts between periods. Watch out: needs a lot of text; learns the quirks of the corpus (names, spellings, OCR); unstable for rare words.

44

J. R. Firth, 1957. Use reflects meaning: words that appear in the same contexts tend to have similar meanings (Wittgenstein: the meaning of a word is its use). Gin and beer are fetched, stood and drunk by the glass, the drop, the pot and the pint. The computer is not told that they are drinks; it groups them because they appear in similar contexts.

45 Predicting the next word: an n-gram model

An n-gram language model: count every trigram, then predict the next word from the two before it. 'A quartern of' (a spirits measure) is followed by gin 44% of the time, then brandy and rum; 'a pint of' by beer, ale, porter, wine and purl. Two points. Words that follow the same phrase tend to be similar: the counts separate spirits from beers (Firth's idea, applied by counting). Predicting the next word is also the task that large language models such as ChatGPT are trained on, using a neural network and much longer contexts. Word2vec, coming up, is also trained by prediction. Old Bailey sample: 111 and 583 occurrences.

46 A co-occurrence matrix

Real counts from the Old Bailey sample: how often each word (rows) appears within 5 words of each context word (columns). Each row is that word's company profile, a list of four numbers: a vector. Gin and beer: drank and pint. Watch and handkerchief: pocket and stole. Pot is in between: a pewter pot is drunk from, and constantly stolen from alehouses. Beer's numbers are bigger than gin's simply because beer is mentioned more often; the next slide deals with that.

47 Cosine similarity

A two-dimensional sketch of the four-column vectors: drinking contexts along the bottom, theft contexts up the side. The angles are drawn to make the groups visible; the cosine numbers on the right are calculated from all four columns of the matrix. Beer's arrow is much longer than gin's because beer is mentioned far more often, but the two point in a similar direction. Watch and handkerchief point in a similar direction to each other, at almost a right angle to the drinks. Similarity should not depend on how common a word is, so we measure the angle: cosine similarity, 1 for the same direction, 0 for a right angle. On all four columns: gin–beer 0.87, gin–pot 0.90, pot–watch 0.26, gin–watch 0.04, watch–handkerchief 0.98.

48 How word2vec learns

A full co-occurrence matrix for the Proceedings would have 20,000 rows and 20,000 columns, mostly zeros. word2vec instead gives each word a short list of numbers (100 in the lab's models) and learns them. Step 1: every word starts with random numbers. Step 2: the model moves along the text, a few words at a time, and takes each word with its neighbours as pairs, e.g. gin–quartern, gin–of, gin–fetch. Step 3: for each real pair it predicts, from the two lists of numbers, how likely the two words are to appear together, and adjusts the numbers so that the prediction is better: the numbers for gin and quartern move closer. It also takes a few random words that did not appear (e.g. handkerchief) and moves those apart. Step 4: this is repeated for every word in the corpus, several times over. Words that appear in similar contexts end up with similar numbers. This version is called skip-gram with negative sampling; the lab's models use it. (CBOW, the other version, predicts the middle word from its neighbours.) Press → to go through the steps.

49 Analogies

The classic example: king − man + woman ≈ queen. Top answers in the Old Bailey models: 1800–1913: queen, carlisle, swinton; 1674–1799: kings, peace, soveraign. In the earlier model 'queen' is not the answer: fewer contexts, different usage (the King's peace). Analogies are unreliable, and the failures can be informative. Ask the room why it fails in one period.

50 Measuring semantic shift

Semantic shift is a change in how a word is used, and so in what it means. To measure it with embeddings: split the corpus into periods, train a separate model for each period, and compare a word's nearest neighbours in each model. If the neighbours change, the word's use changed. Two cautions. The word needs enough uses in each period; with only a few dozen uses the neighbours are noise. And two models trained separately place words in different coordinates, so their numbers cannot be compared directly: compare the neighbour lists, or align the models first (as Hamilton, Leskovec and Jurafsky do). The lab has two models for each corpus, split at 1800.

51 Neighbours of ‘electricity’

Nearest neighbours of 'electricity' in the two word2vec models trained on the Royal Society Corpus (the second lab notebook), split at 1800. Before 1800 it sits among its own specialist vocabulary: electrified, insulated, excited, electrics and non-electrics (the period's names for conductors and insulators). After 1800 its neighbours include voltaic, magneto, magnetism and heat, and it is discussed together with other physical forces (Ørsted, Faraday; W. R. Grove's 'correlation of physical forces', 1846). The bigram table, the collocation chart and the model show the same change.

52 Neighbours in the Dutch parliament

Nearest neighbours in the lab's two Dutch parliament models (1814–1917 and 1918–2012, about 27 million words each). 'Burgers' (citizens): before 1918 its neighbours are staatsburgers (citizens of the state), individuen, medeburgers, ingezetenen (inhabitants); after 1918 they include ondernemers (entrepreneurs), consumenten, belangengroepen (interest groups), omwonenden (local residents) and belastingbetaler (taxpayer). Citizens are discussed alongside other groups whose interests the state has to weigh. 'Gemeenschap' (community, connection): before 1918 it is close to verbinding, verkeer and spoorwegverkeer, because it was often used for a transport connection; after 1918 it is close to unie, eenwording and europese: the European Communities. Two separate models, so we compare neighbour lists. Students can try their own words in the lab.

54 What the models learn

Nearest neighbours from the lab's two Old Bailey models. Names: after 1800, three of the eight nearest neighbours of 'police' are surnames (helson, checkley, langrish), most likely police officers who often gave evidence; 'maroney', Detective Stephen Maroney, is among the neighbours of 'constable'. 57 Summary of the three methods

Recap before the pitfalls. All three methods count words in context and compare: over time, between groups, between periods. Frequency asks how often; association asks in what company; embeddings ask what a word means, from all its contexts at once. Each has its typical trap. And all three end the same way: go back and read.

58 Pitfalls

Dangers and next steps. This part returns to the third question from Part 1: did the corpus change?

59 ‘Asteroid’ in American newspapers, 1820–1900

Ask the room before moving on: what could explain the rise in 'asteroid' from the 1860s onwards, with a peak in the early 1870s? Possible answers: astronomical discoveries, a ship, a person, a horse. Next slide: the answer.

60 The answer: a racehorse

Asteroid was a well-known American racehorse, foaled in 1861 and unbeaten in his races of the mid-1860s. The peak in the early 1870s comes after his racing career, so it is worth reading the articles to see whether they are about the horse, his offspring or something else. Newspapers use words for many reasons: take into account the specific historical circumstances of your sources.

61 ‘Liberty to’ in the Old Bailey

A pitfall of collocation counts, using the liberty example from Part 4. The same check in the Old Bailey: almost every 'liberty to' refers to permission (to go into the yard, to depart, to go on shore). Even 'liberty from' is permission: 'liberty from the master to sleep in the yard' (1807). So a high 'to' share does not show that people favoured positive liberty. Regan makes the same kind of check. Collocation counts show where to read.

62 Trial length in the Proceedings

A pitfall of counting: the source changes. Median words per trial in the Old Bailey sample. The changes in trial length reflect changes in printing: the Proceedings changed from short pamphlets to near-verbatim shorthand records, and were cut down again in the 1890s–1900s. Every count is a count of the source, and only indirectly of the past. Students make this chart in Part 1 of the lab.

63 Other common problems

Visualisations that hide more than they show: word clouds of presidential speeches. Digitisation errors: 'Cafes of Nouns' for 'Cases of Nouns'.
